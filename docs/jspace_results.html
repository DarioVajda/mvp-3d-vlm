J-Lens Readouts

What the Jacobian lens (Anthropic, "A global workspace in language models") reads out of our fine-tuned Qwen3.5-2B: at the image cells the saliency maps marked as influential, at random cells, in the geometry injection itself, and at the answer position. LoRA-only, additive and cross-attention, seed 0; 10 questions, one per VSI question type.

Salient cells read as the objects named in the question; the geometry injection reads as nothing. At the cells the saliency maps picked, the question's own object words (bed, table, TV, sofa) sit near the top of the lens readout (median best rank 17–47 at layer 14), while at random cells they rank in the thousands. Spatial words are no more present at salient cells than at random ones. What geometry adds to a cell (the activation with the injection minus the activation without it) is large, 20–50% of the cell's activation at every layer, but when the lens tries to read it as a word it gets control tokens and sub-word fragments, not "left", "far" or an object name. Only 7–9% of a visual token's activation lies in the part of the space that reads as words at all. If spatial content is readable anywhere, it is at text tokens, which the object-word study now tests.

Methodology, for someone new to this project

Exactly what was done

The question. Our models answer spatial questions about videos. A saliency analysis had already marked which image regions most influence each answer. We wanted to know what the model holds at those regions, in words if possible, and what the added 3D information contributes. Nothing was trained for this; it only reads trained models.

1. The models

The base is Qwen3.5-2B, a small vision-language model with 24 decoder layers and a hidden size of 2,048. A video goes in as 32 separate frames, and each frame becomes 300 visual tokens on a 20×15 grid, so a question has 9,600 visual tokens followed by its text. We read two fine-tuned models from training run v7, both seed 0:

  • LoRA only: small trainable adapters on Qwen, with no 3D input. This is the control.
  • Additive: the same adapters, plus 3D features from VGGT (a frozen 3D reconstruction model). After decoder layers 0, 1 and 2, each visual token has a learned projection of VGGT's features for that same image cell added to it.

The intended checkpoint is step 10,750 of v7, after its learning rate was decayed to zero over 750 steps. The same step's decayed checkpoint produced the saliency maps.

2. The method: a Jacobian lens

The lens is from Anthropic's paper "A global workspace in language models" and asks: what is this internal activation disposed to make the model say? For a chosen layer ℓ it fits one 2,048×2,048 matrix Jℓ, the average Jacobian of the final layer's hidden state with respect to layer ℓ's hidden state. To read an activation h, it computes Jℓh, passes that through the final norm and the model's output layer, and ranks all 151k vocabulary tokens. We reimplemented the estimator from the paper's reference code: one backward pass per output dimension, with the output selected at every target position at once, then an average of the gradients over the source positions. A readout is a disposition, not proof that the model is "thinking" that word.

3. Fitting two lenses per model

  • Text lens (the paper's approach, on far less data). 30 Wikipedia passages of at least 600 characters, cut to 128 tokens, skipping the first 16 tokens and the last. The paper used 1,000.
  • Visual lens (our addition). The paper reports no systematic vision experiments, so a text-fitted lens may not suit visual tokens. This lens is fitted on 3 VSI-Bench videos at 8 frames, one question each, averaging over visual tokens as the source positions only. For the additive model, geometry is on. None of these videos are used in the readouts.

Each model gets its own lenses, because the adapters change the weights. Lenses cover layers 0, 1, 2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20 and 22, with layer 23 as the target.

4. Choosing what to read

  • Questions. One from each of the 10 VSI question types (relative direction counts as three). We preferred questions where additive was right on both seeds and LoRA wrong on both, which gave 5 of the 10; the other 5 had additive right on both seeds and LoRA right on at least one. This deliberately favours questions where geometry helped, so the sample doesn't represent the benchmark.
  • Salient cells. The 5 cells with the highest influence in the additive model's saliency map for that question (gradient × input of the injected geometry, summed over the three injection points). Both models are read at these same cells, so for LoRA they are additive's cells, not its own.
  • Random cells, the control. 5 random cells per question. Anything also seen here isn't specific to influential cells.
  • Text tokens. The last 12 tokens of the prompt, ending at the token where the model starts its answer.

5. What was computed at each position and layer

  • Raw readout: the top 15 tokens.
  • Cell-specific readout (contrast): the lens's log-probabilities minus their average over 64 random cells of that video, to remove what is common to the whole video.
  • Word ranks: the best rank of the question's own words (after removing common words), the best rank of a fixed list of 38 spatial words chosen in advance (left, near, distance, wall and so on), and the rank of the correct answer's first token. Each word counts in its case and leading-space variants, if it is one token.
  • Geometry's contribution (additive only): the same question is run twice with the same weights, once with injection and once without, and the difference hon − hoff is read through the lens.
  • J-space decomposition (visual lens, salient cells and text tokens): the activation minus the video's mean visual token is approximated by at most 25 nonnegative vocabulary directions, chosen greedily and refitted each time. R² is the share of variance explained. The paper uses gradient pursuit, so ours is a similar method, not the same one.

6. Checks

  • Sanity prompts: three text prompts with known associations, read at the token before last.
  • Lens against baseline: on 20 passages not used for fitting, the rank the lens gives the model's own next token, against the plain logit lens (the same readout with no Jacobian). This score can flatter a lens that favours common words.
  • Letter bias: a separate count over all 2,490 multiple-choice questions in the evaluation files. It uses no lens.

7. How to read the numbers

Rank 1 is the top token out of 151k, and lower is better. Medians are over 50 cells (5 per question), and cells from one question aren't independent. So treat the results as descriptive: one seed, 10 hand-ruled questions, and no significance tests on the readouts. Everything here is observational. To show the model uses something, you have to change it and watch the answer change, and that is what the separate object-word study does.

Additive versus cross-attention

Two ways of injecting geometry, read at the same cells and at each model's own cells

The additive arm adds VGGT features into the image tokens at layers 0–2; the cross-attention arm lets the image tokens attend to VGGT features instead. They score the same on the benchmark. Here the lens reads the cross-attention model at two sets of cells: the cells additive's saliency maps picked (so both models are read at identical positions) and the cells the cross-attention model's own saliency maps picked. Of the 50 own cells (5 per question), 18 are also among additive's top 5, so the two models mostly attend to different cells of the same objects.

The geometry injection

Geometry changes the image tokens a lot, but not in words

The question here is simple: what does VGGT geometry add to an image token, and can it be read as language? The test has three steps.

  1. Run the additive model twice on the same video and question: once with the geometry injected (hon), once with the injection switched off (hoff). Everything else is identical, so at each image token the difference hon − hoff is exactly what geometry contributed.
  2. Measure how big that contribution is: the length of the difference divided by the length of the token's activation without geometry. The second column below shows it is 20–50% of the activation at every layer; geometry is not a small nudge.
  3. Ask the lens to read the difference as a word. The lens turns any vector in activation space into a ranking of all 151k vocabulary tokens. If geometry wrote "left", "behind", "far" or "table" into the token, those words should rank near the top. They do not: the best spatial word ranks in the hundreds (third column), the best word from the question in the thousands (fourth), and the tokens that do rank first are control tokens, punctuation and sub-word fragments (last column).

A second, independent check says the same thing from another angle. The "J-space" of the paper is a small set of directions along which activations are readable as words; we fit 25 such directions per layer and asked how much of a salient image token's activation they can rebuild. The answer is a median R² of 0.08 / 0.07 / 0.07 at layers 5 / 11 / 17 for the additive model and 0.10 / 0.07 / 0.08 for LoRA: about 7–9% of the activation lives in the word-readable part of the space, with or without geometry, and the 25 directions themselves have no clear meaning. The paper finds the same ceiling (under 10%) for text tokens in a language model.

What to take from it. Geometry is clearly used, since it changes the token substantially and the scores improve, but at the point where it is injected (layers 0–2, at image tokens) it is not stored in a form that maps onto vocabulary. The model does not "think in words" about the geometry there. Whether a readable spatial signal appears later, at the text tokens that name the objects, is what the object-word study tests.

Checked because of the "C" readouts

Is there an answer-letter bias?

In these readouts C often tops intermediate prompt tokens: it is the first letter at " choices" on all 6 multiple-choice questions at layer 14, in both models, whatever the answer. So we tested whether that reaches the answers, on all 2,490 multiple-choice questions of the full benchmark, treating every letter alike.

  • Answers show no C bias. The intermediate C lean does not reach the output on this evidence. At the true final token, the lens's top letter matches the model's answer on 11 of 12 model-question pairs at layer 20.
  • The one letter effect is in route planning, and it isn't C. Models under-choose A (−4.6 to −10.8 points; the interval excludes zero in 4 of 5 runs). With n = 194 and several letters tested, it is only a lead.
  • What this cannot rule out. Evaluation files hold only the chosen answer, so a small C lean in the logits that rarely flips an answer would not show. The intermediate C lean rests on 6 questions, and it could be an artefact of the lens. Rotating the option letters on the same questions would test this directly; that has not been run.
Reading

What this does and does not show

  • The saliency maps pass a second, independent randomness check. The cells they select are the ones whose activations verbalise the question's objects; random cells do not. The first check was the deletion test on the saliency page (zeroing the top 5% drops confidence 0.62–0.78 vs 0.11–0.12 for a random 5%).
  • "Internal thoughts" at visual cells are object identities, not spatial relations. Nothing here reads as "the chair is left of the table".
  • Geometry's contribution is real but non-verbal at the point of injection. That is consistent with the paper's picture, where the verbalisable workspace is a small slice of activation space, and with our injection being below the paper's workspace layers.
  • Limits. One seed; 10 questions; a 2B model far smaller than those in the paper; lenses fitted on 30 prompts / 3 videos; everything observational, no interventions. The object-word study (recording, probing and patching at the question's object tokens) is the follow-up and is running.

Nothing was trained. Lens fitting and readouts took 19 minutes on one B300. Data: results/jspace_v7/*/readouts.jsonl, sanity.json, lens_quality.json.
