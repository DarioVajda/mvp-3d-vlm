Weird Cases and Patterns

A deep dive into the per-question results of the six v9 models (LoRA only, additive, cross-attention; two seeds each; trunk step 5,750, decayed branch) and the SpatialStack-4B baseline, looking for anything the headline scores hide: answers the models never give, biases in content, scenes that fool everyone, and what more frames buy. Everything here is computed from the stored evaluation files, the same ones as the results CSV; no model was run.

Pattern 1 · route planning

The models never turn back first

Route planning is the weakest task (about 40 points, chance about 30). Splitting the 194 questions by the first move of the correct route shows that the loss is almost entirely one case: when the correct route begins with "Turn Back", every model scores near zero. VSI-590K contains no route-planning questions at all, so this task is answered zero-shot, and "Turn Back" as an opening move seems to be something the models will not produce.

Pattern 2 · relative direction

Geometry helps most when the object is behind you

Relative direction is where additive gains most over LoRA overall (+5 to +9 points). Splitting by the true answer shows the gain is not uniform: on "left", "right", "front-left" and "front-right" it is 2–9 points; on "back", "back-left" and "back-right" it is 15–33 points. These answers are rare in training (10.5% of VSI-590K direction answers are a "back" class) and all models under-predict them, LoRA most.

Pattern 3 · object counting

Most of the counting score comes from answering "2"

53% of counting questions have the answer 2 (training: 41%). The models answer 2 on about 71% of questions. A model that always says 2 would score MRA 60.8; the real models score 70.8–72.6, so the video is worth about 11 points here.

Pattern 4 · absolute distance

LoRA under-estimates; geometry removes the tail

Absolute distance is the task with the second-largest geometry gain (+10 points). The gain comes from the far tail: LoRA answers less than a third of the true distance on 45 questions, additive on 12. A constant answer (the median distance, 1.8 m) scores 31.

Pattern 5 · a scene that fools everyone

A mirrored room produces mirrored answers

Among the 968 relative-direction questions, 29 are answered with the exact mirror of the truth (left for right, back-left for back-right) by all three of our models and by SpatialStack-4B, which uses a different frame pipeline. These are concentrated in 12 scenes; scene 47204578 alone has 8 of them, every direction question asked about it. The video shows why.

Pattern 6 · frame count

128 frames help on long videos and on relative direction, and hurt appearance order

Three models have a complete 128-frame evaluation. The gain over 32 frames is about +1 point overall, but it is not spread evenly: it comes from the longest third of the videos and from relative direction (+3 to +5), while appearance order gets worse for both additive seeds and room size for two of three models. About 8–13% of multiple-choice answers change when frames are added, in both directions.

Pattern 7 · shared difficulty and label noise

The models fail together

Of the 2490 multiple-choice questions, all seven models (six of ours and SpatialStack) are right on 1311 and wrong on 207. If each model's errors were independent at its own accuracy, about 0 questions would be missed by all seven. On 100 questions all seven give the same wrong letter (46 rel distance, 27 rel direction, 19 appearance order, 8 route planning); these are the first candidates for label errors or ambiguous questions, like the mirror scene above.

Pattern 8 · the numbers the models say

Predictions are quantised but not copied from training

Numeric predictions use far fewer distinct values than the truth, and some values recur oddly (additive s0 answers 10.7 m² to 21 room-size questions, 1.5 m to 100 distance questions). We checked whether these are the most common training answers: they are not. Training answers come in mixed units (cm, inches, feet, metres) that the model must convert from the question's unit, and the benchmark's most-asked values are not training modes.

Checks that came back clean

What is not going on

  • No answer-letter bias. Predicted and true letter frequencies agree within ±1 point for every letter and model (full analysis on the J-Lens page). Note that in relative distance the options are always in the same order as the candidates in the question (710 of 710), so a letter bias and a list-position bias would be the same thing; there is neither: additive s0 picks positions 1–4 [170, 185, 172, 183] times against a uniform truth.
  • No "first-named category" shortcut in appearance order. The chosen option starts with the first category named in the question 148 / 141 / 138 times (LoRA / additive / cross-attn) against 138 for the truth, out of 618.
  • No left/right bias. On easy direction questions the models say left and right as often as the truth does (within 4 questions of 217).
  • No portrait-video problem. See the orientation table above.
  • No parsing failures. 0 unparseable answers in 30,780 predictions; no answer longer than 6 characters; numeric answers are full decimals (1.4, 40.6, 81), not single digits.
  • No train/test scene overlap (checked earlier by scene id for ScanNet, ScanNet++ and ARKitScenes).
  • Video length is a mild, consistent effect: longer videos score lower on relative distance (correlation −0.19 with log length), absolute distance (−0.17) and counting (−0.12), not on the other tasks; 128 frames recover part of it.
What this suggests

Where the remaining points are

  1. Route planning, "Turn Back" (~16 points of task score, ~2.0 overall). This is a behavioural gap, not a perception one: the models have the geometry to know the goal is behind them (they get "back" right 50–78% of the time in relative direction) but never emit a route starting with Turn Back. Cheapest tests, in order: (a) prompt-only, add one sentence explaining that Turn Back means a 180° turn, or a one-shot example; (b) look at the first-token logits over the three option letters on the 56 Turn Back questions to see whether Turn Back is a close second or never considered; (c) add synthetic route-planning questions to training, since VSI-590K has none. (a) and (b) are one evaluation job each.
  2. Relative direction, "back" classes. Geometry already helps most here; the residual under-prediction tracks the training prior (10.5% back). Re-weighting or over-sampling "back" answers in training is a one-line change to the data loader.
  3. Counting. The models default to 2 and under-produce 4–6. Worth checking the J-lens / object-word activations at the count token, and whether the frame mix (v9) moved anything here (it did not: v7 and v9 counting are within a point).
  4. Label noise. The 100 same-wrong-letter questions and the 29 all-mirror questions are a fixed cost shared by every model, about 4.0% of multiple-choice. They do not change any comparison between arms, but they cap the achievable score; report scores with and without them if the benchmark is used for a final claim.
